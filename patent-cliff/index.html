Pharma's Patent Cliff Is Coming. Here's What Drugmakers Are Doing About It.

The industry faces its biggest wave of patent expirations in decades. The headline numbers show where the pressure is building, but the real question is whether the defenses will hold.

By Suruchi Sethi, PhD Data as of —

A blockbuster drug can lose most of its sales in a single year. The day a medicine wins approval, a second clock starts ticking toward the day its patents expire, cheaper copies arrive, and revenue that took a decade to build begins to drain away.

That clock is now about to strike for more of the industry at once than at any point in its history. Merck's cancer immunotherapy Keytruda brought in $31.7 billion last year1 and loses U.S. protection in 2028. It is not alone. Nearly every major drugmaker has at least one blockbuster going off patent this decade, and many are bracing for the same few years.

More than $400 billion in cumulative prescription drug sales are at risk from patent expiry through 2030, according to Evaluate's World Preview 2026.2 Extend the horizon to 2033, capturing Keytruda in 2028 and Novo Nordisk's semaglutide in 2032, and the figure rises to more than $500 billion.3

Those numbers measure exposure, not losses. They add up the full sales of drugs losing protection, not the revenue that actually disappears once copies launch. The real question isn't how big the cliff is. It's who falls off it, and who has built something to break the fall.

1.Not every drug falls at the same speed

How quickly sales vanish depends mostly on what kind of drug it is.

Small molecules, the chemically made pills that fill most medicine cabinets, tend to collapse. A 2021 study of 356 such drugs found that brands kept only about 23% of their prescriptions a year after generics launched, and about 18% for drugs that had sold more than $250 million a year.4 When six or more generic makers compete, prices fall more than 95%, according to the FDA.5 And a pharmacist can usually swap in a generic automatically.

Biologics, the more complex drugs made from living cells, have historically faded more slowly. Their copies, called biosimilars, cost more than $100 million and take years to develop, against a few million dollars for a typical generic.6 Pharmacists generally can't substitute them without a special FDA designation, so doctors and insurers switch more gradually.

AbbVie's Humira shows how far a company can push that advantage. Rather than rely on the original molecule patent, AbbVie built a dense thicket around the drug, filing 247 U.S. patent applications, 89% of them after approval, according to the patent-reform group I-MAK.7 The strategy delayed the first U.S. biosimilar until January 2023, years after the core patent expired.

That cushion is thinning. Johnson & Johnson's Stelara lost 41% of its sales in 2025, its first full year against U.S. biosimilars, falling to $6.1 billion from $10.4 billion.8 The combined U.S. franchise of Regeneron's eye drug Eylea fell 27%.9 The erosion is also running faster than Humira's did: a year after Humira's first U.S. biosimilars arrived, they held only about 2% of its market, and it took until late 2025 for branded Humira to be pushed down to about 40%. Stelara, whose biosimilars launched at much steeper discounts, fell to roughly 70% within its first year.10

The belief that biologics erode gently is becoming a riskier bet. Medicare's first negotiated prices took effect in January 2026 for ten high-spending drugs, among them Stelara, Eliquis, Farxiga, Jardiance and Entresto,11 and insurers are more willing to steer patients toward cheaper copies. The headline figures still overstate how fast the money leaves. But a company planning for a gentle decline should test what happens if it instead gets a first year like Stelara's, where sales fell by two-fifths.

2.Some companies are already over the edge

For several drugmakers, the cliff isn't a forecast. It's in the results.

J&J is losing Stelara sales. Amgen's osteoporosis and bone-cancer drugs Prolia and Xgeva have faced biosimilars since June 2025;12 Novartis's heart drug Entresto has had generic rivals since July 2025.13 AstraZeneca's diabetes drug Farxiga got its first U.S. generics in April, and Bristol Myers's blood-cancer drug Revlimid lost the last limits on generic supply in January 2026, and is now eroding.14

Novo Nordisk sits in both camps: generic semaglutide went on sale in India and Canada this year, while its main U.S. patent runs to 2032.

Others are still bracing. Pfizer loses U.S. protection for the breast-cancer drug Ibrance and the prostate-cancer drug Xtandi in 2027. Then comes 2028, the year three giants face competition at once: Keytruda ($31.7 billion in 2025 sales); the blood thinner Eliquis, sold by Bristol Myers and Pfizer ($14.4 billion); and Opdivo ($10 billion). Together they brought in about $56 billion last year. Norstella flags 2028 as the peak, with roughly $100 billion of sales at risk across the industry.3

3.Two ways to measure the damage

Keytruda is the single biggest exposure. The immunotherapy brought in $31.7 billion last year, 48.7% of Merck's total revenue and about 55% of its pharmaceutical sales.1 Merck's annual report says U.S. biosimilars could arrive as early as December 2028; in Europe, its key patent runs to 2031.

Measured by share of sales, Novo Nordisk looks most exposed, but demand for semaglutide is still climbing, and its U.S. patent is years off. In dollar terms, Bristol Myers ranks first, because Eliquis, Opdivo and Revlimid all lose protection within a few years of each other. That view understates how heavily Merck leans on a single drug.

The two measures describe different problems. Bristol Myers has more revenue to replace, spread across several products. Merck has less in dollar terms, but nearly all of it rides on one drug. That makes Keytruda's defense the single biggest swing factor in the industry.

Eli Lilly sits apart. Its tirzepatide, sold as Mounjaro and Zepbound, brought in $36.5 billion last year, 56% of Lilly's revenue,16 and its main patent runs to 2036. Lilly's risk is dependence on one drug, not an approaching cliff.

4.How drugmakers are fighting back

Companies facing the cliff are pulling three levers at once.

Switching patients to shots

Many cancer drugs are given by IV infusion. Newer versions injected under the skin are quicker to administer, and drugmakers are betting that patients who switch will stay with the brand when cheaper IV copies arrive.

The FDA approved Keytruda Qlex, a subcutaneous version, in September 2025 for most of the drug's adult solid-tumor uses.17 It brought in $40 million in its first few months.1 Merck expects 30% to 40% of patients to switch, according to trade-press reports, and Evaluate's consensus forecast calls for about $7.1 billion in Qlex sales by 2032.18

Bristol Myers has Opdivo Qvantig, approved in December 2024, which sold $238 million last year. J&J's Darzalex shows how well the play can work: sales rose 23% to $14.4 billion in 2025, helped by its under-the-skin version, Darzalex Faspro, whose patents J&J holds separately from the ones expiring in 2029.

The shots protect part of the business, not all of it. Faspro worked because doctors adopted it years before patents ran out. Qlex arrived about three years before Keytruda copies can launch in the U.S., and every patient still on the IV version is a potential biosimilar customer. Even at the top of Merck's target, most of the franchise stays exposed. The number to watch is Qlex's share of Keytruda sales each quarter through 2027. Opdivo's shot offers thinner cover, because its patent protection expires around the same time as Opdivo's own, in 2028, so it buys little extra runway.

One asset does buy Merck time: Qlex carries its own U.S. protection to 2043.1 That matters for Merck's other recent win. In August, the Phase 3 INTerpath-001 trial of intismeran autogene, the personalised cancer vaccine it developed with Moderna, met its main endpoints in resected melanoma when given with Keytruda; detailed results are due at the ESMO congress on 24 October.23 A positive readout also supports Keytruda itself, since the vaccine is given alongside it. That helps most before 2028. Afterwards, a cheaper pembrolizumab could take Keytruda's place in the regimen, unless Merck pairs the vaccine with Qlex, whose patents run to 2043.

No single acquisition replaces a drug selling $10 billion to $30 billion a year. The math works only if several midsize products take off at the right time, which is why exposed companies keep dealing, and why dealmaking is unlikely to slow before 2028. The risk is timing: drugs that peak in the early 2030s won't fill a hole that opens in 2028.

Shopping in China

Western drugmakers increasingly look to China for new medicines. Chinese companies were the source of 28% of the experimental drugs big pharma licensed in 2024, according to GlobalData.20 Among larger 2025 deals, those with at least $50 million upfront, the share was 38%, according to DealForma.21 Evaluate expects Chinese drugs to account for more than two-thirds of licensing-deal value this year, up from less than 5% five years ago.2

Merck is the clearest example. In November 2024 it licensed global rights to LM-299, an experimental cancer antibody from China's LaNova Medicines that blocks two targets, PD-1 and VEGF, paying $588 million upfront and up to $2.7 billion more in milestones.22 The drug, now MK-2010, is in early-to-mid-stage trials. It is Merck's bid to own what comes after Keytruda.

Licensing from China is cheaper and faster than discovering drugs from scratch, and dual PD-1/VEGF antibodies are among the most contested ideas in cancer research. But a drug in early trials in 2026 is unlikely to generate meaningful sales before the 2030s. It protects Merck's place in cancer immunotherapy after Keytruda, not its revenue through the cliff. The milestone to watch is a move into late-stage trials. Until then, LM-299 is a hedge, not a bridge.

5.What to watch

  • Keytruda's shot. Qlex's share of Keytruda sales, quarter by quarter.
  • Biosimilar speed. Whether copies of Eylea, which faces more rivals before year-end, and Stelara erode slowly, as Humira's did, or move faster.
  • Washington. The EPIC Act (H.R.1492 / S.832) would give small molecules the same 13 years biologics get before Medicare-negotiated prices apply. The REMEDY Act (S.2620) would limit how many patents a brand can use to trigger an automatic 30-month delay of generic approval. Both are introduced but not passed.
  • Semaglutide abroad. How quickly copies win share in Canada and India, an early read on what 2032 could bring in the U.S.
  • China. Whether licensed drugs reach late-stage trials, and whether politics slows the flow.

The size of the cliff will keep changing with every new forecast. The more useful question is whose defenses are working, and the next two years of sales figures will start to answer it.
